# Work type: Creative

## What the client is buying
Creative concepts that grow from a real audience insight, can be produced with the resources available, and carry from the ad to the landing page without breaking the promise. The client is buying a choice of distinct routes, a clear recommendation, and what is needed to make it: copy direction, a landing page specification a designer and developer can build from, and an asset matrix a production team can schedule. The work must respect brand rules and claims review, and every concept must be testable.

## What intake must pull out of the brief
- The product or offer, the single thing the client wants people to believe or do, and the proof behind it.
- The audience and what is known about them: needs, objections, language, and where they are in the decision.
- The channels and placements the creative must run in, with formats and aspect ratios where known.
- Brand guidelines, tone of voice, existing campaign lines, assets that must be used and things to avoid.
- Claims rules: regulated category, substantiation required, legal review time, platform policy risks.
- Production capacity and budget: who designs, who shoots or edits video, UGC or creator access, stock and licensing, turnaround times.
- Landing page context: the existing page or site, CMS, developer capacity, form or checkout, tracking.
- The KPI the creative is judged on (CTR, thumbstop rate, conversion rate, CPA, brand lift) and how tests will be read.
- Deadlines: concept review, final assets, launch, and any refresh dates.

## What each seat's kickoff should focus on
- creative-strategist: two or three distinct insights, each with a proposition and a concept route, the reason each could work and the reason each could fail.
- copywriter: headlines, hooks and CTAs per route within platform character limits, and every claim that needs substantiation.
- ui: visual direction per route, how it sits within the brand guidelines, and the template system for ads and pages.
- ux: the journey from each ad to the page, the first screen, the form or checkout, and the friction to remove.
- developer: what the landing page needs to be built in the available time and CMS, the page speed budget, and the tracking events.
- creative-ops: the asset matrix by concept, channel, format and size, production hours, lead times, naming convention and QA steps.
- Ad seats: format and placement needs, the creative volume per campaign or ad set, refresh cadence, and platform policy risks for each route.
- measurement: how concepts will be tested and compared, and which events the landing page must fire.
- Usually essential: creative-strategist, copywriter, ui, ux, creative-ops and client-lead. Add developer when a page is being built, the ad seats for channels in scope, and measurement when a test plan is required.

## Deliverable outline
1. Executive summary: the insight, the recommended concept, why it wins, what it takes to produce, and the decisions needed.
2. Audience and context: who we are talking to, what they believe now, their objections, and the competitive creative landscape.
3. Insight and proposition: the insight in one sentence with its evidence, the single-minded proposition, and the proof points behind it.
4. Concepts: two or three distinct routes, each with a name, core idea, hooks and headlines, visual direction, one example execution per key channel, and its strengths and risks.
5. Recommendation and testing plan: the recommended route, what is tested against what, the metric, the budget or volume needed to read the test, and the decision rule.
6. Copy direction: headlines, primary text and CTAs per platform within character limits, tone notes, and claims flagged for review.
7. Ad-to-page journey: for each entry point, the ad's promise, the page's first screen, the next step, and how message and visual match are kept.
8. Landing page specification: sections in order with purpose and content, form or checkout fields, states and errors, accessibility, performance budget, tracking events, build owner and live date.
9. Asset matrix and production plan: a table of every asset by concept, channel, placement, format, size and variant, with specs, naming convention, owner, hours, review steps and due dates.
10. Owners and responsibilities: who owns concept, copy, design, build, production, trafficking and QA, with client-side approvers and dates.
11. Risks and mitigations: claims, policy, brand, production and performance risks, each with a mitigation and owner.
12. Assumptions: assumptions about the audience, capacity, timelines and performance, with their sources.
13. Decisions and open questions for the client: the route choice, claims and approvals needed, each with a recommendation and needed-by date, followed by open questions.

## Acceptance criteria
- Each concept traces to a stated insight, and the concepts are genuinely distinct: a different insight or proposition, not the same idea restyled.
- Every claim in the copy is either substantiated by the brief or flagged for legal review, and the review time appears in the schedule.
- Copy fits each platform's character limits for the placements listed.
- Every entry point in the ad-to-page journey maps to a landing page whose first screen repeats the ad's promise.
- The landing page specification lists every section, form field and tracking event, and its live date fits the stated developer capacity.
- The asset matrix covers every channel, placement and format in scope, its count matches what the ad seats said they need, and every row has an owner and due date.
- Total production hours fit the stated capacity, or the gap is called out with options.
- The testing plan names the metric, the comparison and the decision rule.
- Nothing contradicts the brand guidelines or the brief's must-avoid list.
