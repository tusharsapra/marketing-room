# Work type: Custom

## What the client is buying
Whatever the user describes that is not a campaign, audit, pitch or creative job: a channel strategy, a measurement roadmap, a market-entry plan, a team structure, a testing programme, a response to a sudden performance drop. The client is buying a clear answer to their question, a plan to act on it, and honesty about what is uncertain. The Project Head defines the deliverable from the user's description at intake and holds the room to it.

## What intake must pull out of the brief
- The question or task in the user's own words, and a one-sentence definition of done that the Project Head will review against.
- Who the deliverable is for and what they will do with it: decide, approve, brief a team, present.
- The scope: what is in, what is out, and which standard elements (budget, timeline, measurement, creative) apply to this task.
- The standard facts that do apply: budget, timeline, markets, channels, current state, constraints.
- Data, files or links supplied, and what the work must conclude from them.
- The format, length and structure the user expects, including any headings they asked for.
- Deadlines and approvers.

## What each seat's kickoff should focus on
- Every seat: its answer to the user's question from its own discipline, the evidence for it, what it would recommend doing, and what it cannot judge.
- Every seat: whether it belongs in the room at all. A seat with nothing material to add says so in one line.
- Ad seats, measurement and client-lead: numbers and dates only where the task involves them, with the maths shown.
- Every seat: where it expects to disagree with another seat, so conflicts surface early.
- Usually essential: client-lead always. The Project Head casts the rest from the task description and records the reason for each seat.

## Deliverable outline
The Facilitator may rename the headings between "Executive summary" and "Owners and responsibilities" to fit the task, and may merge or split them as long as the outline stays within 9 to 14 headings, but must keep the five mandatory headings exactly as written and in this order: "Executive summary" first, then "Owners and responsibilities", "Risks and mitigations", "Assumptions", and "Decisions and open questions for the client" last.

1. Executive summary: the question, the answer in two or three sentences, what we recommend doing next, and the decisions needed.
2. Task and scope: the task as the user described it, what is in and out of scope, and the definition of done.
3. Context and current state: the facts from the brief and the supplied data that the answer rests on.
4. Analysis: what the room found, organised by the questions the task raises, with the evidence for each point.
5. Options considered: the realistic options, with the benefits, costs and risks of each.
6. Recommendation: the chosen option and why it beats the others.
7. Plan and timeline: the steps to act on the recommendation, with dates, dependencies and resources.
8. Measurement and success criteria: how the user will know it worked, the metrics, and when to check them.
9. Owners and responsibilities: each workstream or action with its accountable seat, the client-side owner and the due date.
10. Risks and mitigations: each risk with its impact, early warning sign, mitigation and owner.
11. Assumptions: every assumption the answer depends on, with its source.
12. Decisions and open questions for the client: each decision needed with a recommendation and needed-by date, followed by open questions.

## Acceptance criteria
- The deliverable answers the question recorded at intake, and the executive summary states that answer directly.
- The definition of done from intake is met, point by point.
- The five mandatory headings appear exactly as written, with "Executive summary" first and "Decisions and open questions for the client" last.
- Any heading, section or format the user asked for is present.
- Every recommended action has an owner and a date, or a stated reason it has neither.
- Every figure shows its source or calculation, and nothing is presented as fact unless it comes from the brief, the data or a named source.
- Options considered includes at least one option the room rejected, with the reason.
- Out-of-scope items are not planned for, only noted where they affect the answer.
