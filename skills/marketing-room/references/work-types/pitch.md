# Work type: Pitch

## What the client is buying
In a pitch the prospect is buying confidence: evidence that the agency understood the brief better than the other bidders, has a credible plan to reach the target, and will be good to work with. The response must answer the RFP's questions, in its structure where one is given, with a clear point of view and honesty about what the agency does not yet know. Nothing in it can be invented, because the prospect will check.

## What intake must pull out of the brief
- The prospect, their offer, and the problem they say they want solved, in their words.
- Every question, required section, format rule, page or slide limit and scoring criterion in the RFP, so the response can map to it one to one.
- Submission deadline, presentation date, decision date and expected start date.
- Budget or budget range, and whether it covers media, fees, production or all three. If none is given, record that.
- The KPI or success definition the prospect will judge the winning agency by.
- What the prospect has now: incumbent agency, current channels, tracking status, and any past performance they shared.
- The evaluators and decision makers, and what each is likely to care about.
- Facts about our agency that the user has supplied: team, relevant experience, case studies, tools, certifications, fees. Record only what the user states; everything else is unknown.
- Mandatories: required formats, contract terms, conflicts of interest, regulated-category rules, and anything the prospect said to avoid.

## What each seat's kickoff should focus on
- Every seat: its read of the prospect's real problem, one sharp insight or opportunity from its discipline, and what it would do in the first 90 days.
- Every seat: the questions it would ask the prospect in a clarification or chemistry session.
- Every seat: no claims of experience, results or credentials the user has not supplied. Mark each gap as "[agency to supply: what is needed]".
- Ad seats: which channels earn a place in the recommendation, an indicative split with its logic, and forecasts as ranges with the assumptions stated, never as point estimates.
- creative-strategist and copywriter: the strategic platform or big idea, and one or two example executions that show it working.
- measurement: how success will be proven, and what must be fixed in the prospect's tracking in month one.
- client-lead: the RFP's scoring criteria and where the response answers each, the proposed team structure, cadence and reporting, and the commercial assumptions.
- Usually essential: client-lead, creative-strategist, measurement and the ad seats for channels named in the RFP. Add seo, ux or developer when the RFP asks for them.

## Deliverable outline
1. Executive summary: the prospect's challenge in one line, our point of view, the headline recommendation and the expected outcome as a range.
2. Our understanding of the brief: the prospect's goal, audience, constraints and success criteria restated in our own words, showing a close reading of the RFP.
3. The real challenge: the insight or reframing behind our answer, with its evidence from the brief or supplied data.
4. Proposed approach: the strategy, the principles behind it, and how it meets each of the prospect's stated requirements.
5. Channel and creative plan: the recommended channels and why, the indicative budget split, the creative platform and example executions.
6. Measurement and success criteria: the KPIs, how each is tracked, the month-one tracking fixes, and the reporting the prospect will receive.
7. First 90 days: onboarding, audit, launch and optimisation steps week by week, with what the prospect needs to provide and when.
8. Team and ways of working: roles and responsibilities, meeting and reporting cadence, approval flow and escalation route.
9. Commercials: the fee model and any figures the user supplied, otherwise clearly marked placeholders, plus the scope assumptions behind them.
10. Why us: an honest case built only from facts the user supplied about the agency, with placeholders where evidence is needed and no invented clients, results, awards, certifications or case studies.
11. Owners and responsibilities: who owns each workstream on our side and on the prospect's side, from kickoff to the end of the first 90 days.
12. Risks and mitigations: delivery and performance risks, each with a mitigation and owner.
13. Assumptions: every assumption behind the approach, the split and the forecast ranges, with its source.
14. Decisions and open questions for the client: questions for the prospect before or at the presentation, and the decisions they must make for work to start, each with a recommendation where we have one.

## Acceptance criteria
- Every question and required section in the RFP is answered, either by following the RFP's structure or through an explicit map from RFP item to response section.
- The response meets every format rule in the brief: length limits, required sections, submission format.
- "Why us" and "Team and ways of working" contain no client names, results, awards, certifications or team biographies that the user did not supply.
- Every gap in agency facts uses the form "[agency to supply: ...]" so the user can find and fill each one before submission.
- "Our understanding of the brief" contains no fact that is absent from the brief or the supplied materials.
- Forecasts are ranges with stated assumptions, and none is presented as a guarantee.
- The budget split adds up to the stated budget or range, or is given in percentages when no budget was stated.
- The proposed approach addresses each of the prospect's stated success criteria by name.
- The first 90 days starts from the expected start date and lists what the prospect must provide.
